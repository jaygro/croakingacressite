NRCS grants: see everything that's possible, then ask for it.

The Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) is the part of USDA that helps farms of every size fix soil, water, and plant problems. Many small farms apply for one thing, like a high tunnel, and never hear that NRCS can also help pay for irrigation, rainwater capture, soil and fertility planning, pest management, wind protection, and more. This guide shows the full range and helps you turn your ideas into a clear request.

One thing to know up front: NRCS mostly pays you back after the work is done and inspected, so it works more like cost-share than a grant check. New words? There's a short glossary at the bottom.

This is an independent guide, not an official USDA or NRCS page. Programs, practice lists, payment rates, and deadlines change, and they differ by state. Always confirm details with your local NRCS office before you plan around anything here.

New to USDA programs? Before you pick practices, take the 2-minute pre-check. You'll need a few things in place first, like a farm number from the Farm Service Agency and proof you can make changes to the land. Many people sort these out in one office visit. Some can't yet, and we'll show you other options.

Before you apply: is this for me?

NRCS (the Natural Resources Conservation Service, part of USDA) can help pay for conservation work on farms of almost any size. Before it can pay you, a few things have to be true about you and your land. Answer these to see where you stand. Not sure about one? Skip it. The office can answer it, and your answers stay in your browser.

    Usually fine

    • Small plots, backyards, community gardens, and urban lots. NRCS says size and location aren't the test.
    • Growing only for your family or neighbors. You don't have to sell anything.
    • No crop history. You can self-certify what you grow on the application, and NRCS may confirm with a visit.
    • Watering with a hose or watering can, or with city water or rainwater. Any legal method counts as irrigation history.

    Check these first

    • Nothing growing or raised yet. You need to be producing something when you apply.
    • Renting without a written lease, or a landowner who won't OK changes.
    • Public, HOA, or shared land where you don't decide what gets built.
    • Land already in the Conservation Reserve Program (CRP) or a wetland easement, or land that's permanently underwater.
    • Irrigation practices on ground that hasn't been watered in 2 of the last 5 years.
    • Average adjusted gross income (AGI) over $900,000, unless 75% or more of it comes from farming.
    • Zoning, permits, or HOA rules that ban structures like high tunnels.

    One more thing: NRCS, not FSA, decides whether your land is eligible. Having a farm number is required, but it doesn't guarantee the land qualifies.

    What to bring to your first visit

    • Your Social Security number, or an EIN if you farm as a business
    • Your deed, or a written lease if you rent, plus your landowner's contact information
    • A rough map or photos of where you grow, and what you grow or raise there now
    • Business documents, such as articles of organization, if you farm as an LLC or other entity

    Think in problems, not shopping lists

    NRCS doesn't fund wish lists. It helps pay for conservation practices that fix a "resource concern" on your land, such as soil health, erosion, water supply and quality, plant health, pests, or wildlife habitat. Your request works best when it says what's going wrong or what you want to improve, and then names the practices that would address it.

    1. Name the problem

    Wet spot in the field, plants stressed by dry spells, cold nights cutting your season short, weeds eating your time, soil that has worn out.

    2. Think in systems

    A tunnel pairs with irrigation, rain gutters, and raised beds. A soil-building plan pairs cover crops, compost, and testing. Practices that work together often make a stronger request.

    3. Bring a draft to NRCS

    A conservation planner works with you to build the real plan. Walking in with a clear list and notes saves time and shows them what you actually need.

    How the process works

    The details vary by state and year, but the path for EQIP (the Environmental Quality Incentives Program, the main NRCS program for working farms) generally looks like this.

    1. Call your USDA Service Center and ask for two appointments, one with FSA and one with NRCS.They're usually in the same building. Tell NRCS you're interested in EQIP. Talking to NRCS and getting a planning visit is free and doesn't need a farm number. You need the farm number only to get paid.
    2. Set up your records with the Farm Service Agency (FSA).FSA gives your land a farm and tract number and makes a map of it. Bring the items from What to bring. Expect to fill out AD-2047 (customer information), CCC-902 (farm operating plan), CCC-941 (income certification), and AD-1026 (highly erodible land and wetland certification). If your land has never been farmed or sits in town, FSA may need to map it first, so ask how long setup will take and book it early.
    3. Develop a conservation plan with your planner.This is where your worksheet helps. You and the planner pick practices that address your resource concerns.
    4. Complete the application and paperwork.The NRCS application is form CPA-1200. NRCS also checks that your FSA records, tax ID, and proof of land control (a deed or lease) are in place. If you lease, landowner permission can matter for some practices.
    5. Meet the ranking date.NRCS accepts applications year-round, but funding decisions are made at set ranking dates, and applications are scored competitively. Each state sets its own cutoff dates, listed on the NRCS application cutoff dates page (linked below). Missing a date means waiting for the next cycle.
    6. If you're selected, sign a contract. Don't start the work first.Starting a practice before NRCS signs your contract can make that practice ineligible for payment. A waiver to start early exists, but it has to be requested in writing and granted beforehand.
    7. Build it to NRCS standards, then get paid.NRCS typically pays after the work is done and inspected, so plan to cover the cost first. Beginning, veteran, limited resource, and other historically underserved producers can ask for an advance payment and may get higher payment rates. How USDA defines these groups changed for some programs in 2025, so ask how your office defines them now.

    Eligibility basics generally include: you control or own eligible land, meet adjusted gross income limits, comply with highly erodible land and wetland conservation rules, and develop a conservation plan. Already doing good conservation work? Ask about the Conservation Stewardship Program (CSP), which pays for maintaining and building on it.

    Questions to ask your conservationist

    These come along in your summary too.

      If NRCS isn't a fit right now

      Maybe you rent without a lease, haven't started growing yet, or your project doesn't fit EQIP. You still have options, and most of them are free.

      Your soil and water conservation district

      Every county has one, often in the same USDA office. Many run their own cost-share, rain barrel, cover crop, or urban programs with simpler rules. Find yours through the National Association of Conservation Districts or your state conservation agency.

      Cooperative Extension

      Your state's land-grant university has a county office with soil testing, small farm and urban ag programs, and Master Gardener help.

      ATTRA

      A free sustainable agriculture helpline and library of guides, run by the National Center for Appropriate Technology. Find it at attra.ncat.org.

      SARE grants

      Sustainable Agriculture Research and Education offers farmer and rancher grants for trying new practices, run by region. See sare.org.

      State and local programs

      Check your state department of agriculture and your city or county for specialty crop, urban ag, stormwater credit, or rain barrel rebate programs.

      NRCS help without a contract

      An NRCS planner can still visit and help you write a conservation plan. Then you're ready to apply when your situation changes.

      Good to know

      Is a high tunnel the only thing worth asking for?

      No. NRCS's high tunnel initiative practice list also includes irrigation, roof runoff structures, raised beds, low tunnels, mulching, nutrient and pest management, and more. Tunnels get the attention, but the supporting practices are often what make a farm work.

      Will everything I tick get funded?

      No. Practices have to address a documented resource concern, fit NRCS design standards, be on your state's current list, and rank well against other applicants. Think of your picks as a starting conversation, not a promise.

      What do practices pay?

      Payment rates are set by state and year, and some practices have small-scale scenarios. We don't list dollar amounts here because they go stale quickly. Ask your local office for your state's current payment schedule.

      I'm a small or urban farm. Is there special help?

      Many state NRCS offices have a page for small and urban farms and an urban or small-scale conservationist, so check yours. NRCS also describes targeted funding and higher rates for beginning, veteran, limited resource, and other historically underserved producers, and an Organic Initiative. Ask about each when you meet.

      Do I have to pay up front?

      Usually, yes. NRCS pays after your practice is built to its standards and inspected. Beginning, veteran, limited resource, and other historically underserved producers can ask for an advance of at least 50% of a practice's payment, which has to be spent within 90 days. Signing a contract also means keeping each practice working for its full lifespan, often several years, so think about your lease length or any plans to move.

      I just have a garden. Does that count?

      It can. NRCS says size and location don't decide eligibility, and you don't have to sell anything. You do need to be growing something, control the land, and have a resource problem a practice would fix. Take the pre-check to see where you stand.

      I rent. Can I apply?

      Yes, with a written lease or agreement that covers the contract years. Some practices, especially structures, need your landowner's permission, so bring their contact information.

      Glossary

      USDA
      U.S. Department of Agriculture. NRCS and FSA are both part of it.
      NRCS
      Natural Resources Conservation Service. Plans conservation work with you and pays part of the cost.
      FSA
      Farm Service Agency. Keeps farm records and handles the eligibility paperwork.
      Service Center
      Your local USDA office. NRCS, FSA, and the conservation district are often in one building.
      Farm and tract number
      FSA's ID for your land. It belongs to the land, not to you.
      EQIP
      Environmental Quality Incentives Program. The main NRCS cost-share program.
      CSP
      Conservation Stewardship Program. For operations already doing conservation.
      CRP
      Conservation Reserve Program. An FSA program that takes cropland out of production.
      Practice
      A specific, numbered piece of work NRCS knows how to design and pay for, like "325 High tunnel."
      Resource concern
      The problem a practice fixes, such as erosion, water, soil health, or pests.
      Ranking and cutoff date
      Applications are scored against each other in batches. Each state sets its own cutoff dates.
      Payment schedule
      Your state's list of what each practice pays this year.
      AGI
      Adjusted gross income from your tax return. The limit is a $900,000 average.
      HEL and WC
      Highly erodible land and wetland conservation rules. You certify them on form AD-1026.
      CPA-1200
      The NRCS application form.
      EIN
      Employer Identification Number, a tax ID for a business. Individuals use their Social Security number.
      Lifespan
      How long you agree to keep a practice working after you're paid.
