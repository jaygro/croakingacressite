NRCS grants: see everything that's possible, then ask for it.

Many small farms apply for one thing, like a high tunnel, and never hear that NRCS can also help pay for irrigation, rainwater capture, soil and fertility planning, pest management, wind protection, and more. This guide shows the full range and helps you turn your ideas into a clear request.

This is an independent guide, not an official USDA or NRCS page. Programs, practice lists, payment rates, and deadlines change, and they differ by state. Always confirm details with your local NRCS office before you plan around anything here.

Think in problems, not shopping lists

NRCS doesn't fund wish lists. It helps pay for conservation practices that fix a "resource concern" on your land, such as soil health, erosion, water supply and quality, plant health, pests, or wildlife habitat. Your request works best when it says what's going wrong or what you want to improve, and then names the practices that would address it.

1. Name the problem

Wet spot in the field, plants stressed by dry spells, cold nights cutting your season short, weeds eating your time, soil that has worn out.

2. Think in systems

A tunnel pairs with irrigation, rain gutters, and raised beds. A soil-building plan pairs cover crops, compost, and testing. Practices that work together often make a stronger request.

3. Bring a draft to NRCS

A conservation planner works with you to build the real plan. Walking in with a clear list and notes saves time and shows them what you actually need.

How the process works

The details vary by state and year, but the path for EQIP (the main NRCS program for working farms) generally looks like this.

  1. Contact your local USDA Service Center.Tell them you're interested in EQIP. You can also start through farmers.gov. A conservation planner will check your eligibility and learn about your land.
  2. Get a farm number from the Farm Service Agency (FSA).NRCS requires one for financial assistance. FSA is usually in the same building as NRCS. If you're new to USDA programs, make this appointment early.
  3. Develop a conservation plan with your planner.This is where your worksheet helps. You and the planner pick practices that address your resource concerns.
  4. Complete the application and paperwork.This includes forms CPA-1200 and AD-1026, a tax ID (SSN or EIN), and proof you control the land, like a deed or a lease. If you lease, landowner permission can matter for some practices.
  5. Meet the ranking date.NRCS accepts applications year-round, but funding decisions are made at set ranking dates, and applications are scored competitively. Each state sets its own cutoff dates, listed on the NRCS application cutoff dates page (linked below). Missing a date means waiting for the next cycle.
  6. If you're selected, sign a contract. Don't start the work first.Starting a practice before NRCS signs your contract can make that practice ineligible for payment. A waiver to start early exists, but it has to be requested in writing and granted beforehand.
  7. Build it to NRCS standards, then get paid.NRCS typically pays after the work is done and inspected. Some producers qualify for advance payment, and NRCS describes higher payment rates for beginning, veteran, limited resource, and socially disadvantaged producers, so ask which applies to you.

Eligibility basics generally include: you control or own eligible land, meet adjusted gross income limits, comply with highly erodible land and wetland conservation rules, and develop a conservation plan. Already doing good conservation work? Ask about the Conservation Stewardship Program (CSP), which pays for maintaining and building on it.

Questions to ask your conservationist

These come along in your summary too.

    Good to know

    Is a high tunnel the only thing worth asking for?

    No. NRCS's high tunnel initiative practice list also includes irrigation, roof runoff structures, raised beds, low tunnels, mulching, nutrient and pest management, and more. Tunnels get the attention, but the supporting practices are often what make a farm work.

    Will everything I tick get funded?

    No. Practices have to address a documented resource concern, fit NRCS design standards, be on your state's current list, and rank well against other applicants. Think of your picks as a starting conversation, not a promise.

    What do practices pay?

    Payment rates are set by state and year, and some practices have small-scale scenarios. We don't list dollar amounts here because they go stale quickly. Ask your local office for your state's current payment schedule.

    I'm a small or urban farm. Is there special help?

    Many state NRCS offices have a page for small and urban farms and an urban or small-scale conservationist, so check yours. NRCS also describes targeted funding and higher rates for historically underserved producers, and an Organic Initiative. Ask about each when you meet.
